Section 00 · Orientation

Read this first

This is not a course about a video game. It's a course about attention arbitrage — how to position a faceless content operation in front of the single largest, most predictable spike in global consumer attention this decade, and convert it into revenue before, during, and after it peaks.

On November 19, 2026, roughly the same thing happens that happened with the Super Bowl, the Barbie release, and the launch of ChatGPT — except bigger, and unlike those, you already know the exact date.

That's the entire edge. A scheduled tsunami with a published arrival time and months of lead.

What this will not do

  • Tell you what's in the game. Nobody knows. Anyone selling you leaks is selling you a lawsuit.
  • Promise a dollar amount. Anyone who does is lying or reckless.
  • Teach fake-leak content. That model is already dead — Section 02 shows why, with receipts. It's the most valuable part of this course.

What it will do

  • Show you seven content lanes that reliably monetize around an event this size, ranked by speed to first dollar.
  • Give you a production system that lets one person publish 5–15 pieces a day without burning out.
  • Build all of it on a legal footing that doesn't get deleted the week it starts earning.
  • Hand you a day-by-day plan from today through December 31.
  • Turn a 90-day spike into an asset that still pays in 2028.
How to use this

Don't read it cover to cover and then start. Read 01 and 02 today. Execute 03 and 04 this week. Mark each section complete as you go — the progress bar at the top is your actual build progress, not your reading progress.

Section 01

The Window

Content businesses die from one thing more than any other: unpredictable demand. An event this size inverts that completely.

Demand you can actually forecast

Predictable in timing. November 19, 2026. Preload opens November 12. Marketing beats — trailers, gameplay reveals, review embargo, streamer early access — land in a compressed window before that. You can build inventory in advance of demand you know is coming.

Predictable in volume. Pre-orders opened June 25 at $79.99 standard / $99.99 ultimate. The publisher has publicly tied record fiscal-year revenue to this launch. There's no PC release at launch — which means an enormous secondary audience who'll spend the whole window watching rather than playing. Watching means consuming content. That's your audience.

Predictable in shape. We know what people want around a launch this size because it happens the same way at every scale:

PhaseWhat people wantWhat wins
90–30 days outSpeculation, comparison, hypeAnalysis, countdowns, "everything we know"
30–7 days outPreparation, decisionsWhich edition, which console, is it worth it
Launch weekOrientation, help, reactionGuides, tips, first-hours, reactions
7–90 days afterMastery, discoveryDeep guides, methods, secrets, best-of lists
90+ daysLong-tail utilityEvergreen guides, "still worth it in 2027"

Each row is a different content product. Most people make one and quit. You're going to run the whole arc.

The four phases

Phase 1 — Build (now → Oct 15)

Your job right now is not to make money. It's to accumulate three assets: aged accounts with a topical history the algorithm trusts; a tested format, because you don't want to be discovering what works on November 12; and an owned audience — email, Discord, SMS — because platforms giveth and taketh away.

If you do nothing else: post daily, find one format with consistent watch time, start capturing emails.

Phase 2 — Surge (Oct 15 → Nov 11)

Marketing ramps. Search volume climbs steeply. You go from 2 posts a day to 6–10, launch your monetization offers, and start paid traffic if you have budget.

Phase 3 — Detonation (Nov 12 → Dec 31)

Preload on the 12th, launch on the 19th. Every metric you care about at its ceiling. This is the harvest.

The rule of Phase 3

Build nothing. Sell everything. It must all already exist.

Phase 4 — Harvest (Jan 2027 →)

The spike decays but doesn't disappear. Online modes mature, updates ship, the PC port gets announced and released (historically a 6–18 month lag), DLC arrives. Your channels are now aged, monetized, and authoritative in a category with years of runway. That's where the actual asset value is — see Section 09.

The uncomfortable math

You'll make better decisions with real numbers, so here they are.

Platform creator payouts are low. Short-form programs pay roughly $0.02–$1.00 per 1,000 views depending on platform, audience geography, and program. Gaming audiences skew young and international, pushing you toward the low end. A million short-form views might pay $30–$400. Real money, but not the business.

The business is what you do with the traffic. Every serious faceless operator earns most revenue elsewhere: affiliates, their own products, brand deals, and traffic redirected somewhere worth more. Section 07 covers all seven rails.

Expectation setting

Most accounts make $0 for the first 30–45 days. That's normal and it's not a signal to quit — it's what accumulation looks like. Nobody can tell you which outcome you'll get. Volume, consistency, and the legal discipline in Section 02 are the three variables you actually control.

Section 02 · Read this twice

The Legal Moat

Worth more than the rest of this course combined. Not because it makes you money directly — because it stops you losing everything in the exact month the money arrives.

The business model that just died

Between 2024 and 2026 an entire cottage industry grew around AI-generated "leaked footage" — hyper-realistic clips passed off as real leaks. Millions of views. It looked like free money.

That industry is being systematically dismantled right now.

WhenWhat happened
Early 2026Takedowns issued against AI-generated videos made with a generative world model that produced game-like interactive environments.
April 2026A prominent fan account received a cease-and-desist and issued a public apology on April 24, acknowledging the AI content used copyrighted material and trademarks and risked confusing fans into thinking it was real.
Mid-2026Mass DMCA takedowns across YouTube and X. Videos trending one day showed copyright-holder notices the next. Large channels targeted, not just individual uploads.
OngoingLong enforcement history against mods, ports, VR adaptations — including a fan map project built only from trailer analysis and speculation.

Why "but the AI made it" is not a defense

Copyright protects the expression. Trademark protects the brand identifiers. An AI image containing none of the original's pixels can still infringe because it:

  • Uses protected trademarks — series name, logo, distinctive title typography, character names.
  • Creates consumer confusion, the core trademark harm. If a viewer thinks they're seeing official material, that's precisely the injury the law exists to prevent.
  • Can be a derivative work built on copyrighted source material.

The DMCA lets a rights holder demand removal of content that creates confusion or damages the brand, and platforms comply first and ask later. Add that the publisher's CEO stated publicly in February 2026 that generative AI has no part in the studio's own development and its worlds are handcrafted — that's a brand-integrity position, which means AI imitation is treated as a brand threat, not a fan tribute.

The timing problem

Enforcement gets more aggressive as launch approaches, because the company is protecting the most commercially critical marketing pipeline in its history. Build on fake leaks and the most likely month to get deleted is the exact month the money shows up. A hundred days of work, wiped at the finish line.

The compliant play

Here's the good news: the enforcement is aimed at a narrow target. It hits content that imitates the product or impersonates official material. It does not touch commentary, news, analysis, education, or original creative work that merely discusses the subject.

You're going to build in the safe lane — and the safe lane is less competitive precisely because everyone else is still chasing traffic that's about to evaporate.

The eight rules of the moat

Rule 01

Never imply your visuals are real

Confusion is the injury. If a reasonable viewer could think your AI clip is actual footage, you're in the danger zone. Kill every LEAKED / REAL FOOTAGE / THIS JUST DROPPED framing — it's both the legal risk and the exact framing the takedowns targeted.

Rule 02

No logo, no title typography, no official art

The wordmark and that distinctive lettering are trademarks. Not in thumbnails, banners, or ad creative. You can write the game's name in a normal font — nominative reference — without recreating the brand's visual identity.

Rule 03

Don't name your channel or product after the IP

"GTA6Leaks" is a channel that gets taken down and a brand you can never sell. Build a name you own. This course is called The November Window, not "The GTA 6 Course," for exactly this reason.

Rule 04

Label AI content as AI

Every major platform now has AI-disclosure requirements; failing to disclose is an independent violation stacked on top of any IP issue. Use the built-in label. It costs nothing and removes a whole category of risk — and it hurts performance far less than people assume once you're framed as commentary rather than leak.

Rule 05

Add commentary, don't repost

Transformation is your friend. Official footage with your original analysis over it sits in a very different position than a silent reupload. Original voiceover, original script, original conclusions, meaningful reduction in source material used.

Rule 06

Build on generic, not specific

An AI-generated neon Miami street at dusk is a generic aesthetic. A recognizable in-game location with the game's characters is protected expression. Learn the difference. You can evoke vibe — palm trees, sunset, 80s neon, Florida — all day. Nobody owns Miami at night.

Rule 07

Diversify accounts and platforms from day one

Three accounts, three platforms. One strike shouldn't end your business. Basic hygiene, and almost nobody does it.

Rule 08

Own your audience

Email list from week one. If every platform account vanished tonight, a list of 5,000 buyers is still a business.

The five lanes enforcement doesn't touch

  1. News and analysis — official announcements, trailers, pre-order data, industry impact. Journalism and commentary.
  2. Preparation and buying guidance — which edition, which console, hardware, storage, budgeting, upgrade decisions.
  3. Guides and mastery — post-launch how-to. Enormous, durable, highly monetizable.
  4. Culture and reaction — the phenomenon itself: economics, delays, other studios moving release dates.
  5. Original creative work — your own aesthetic, your own worlds, the vibe without the IP. Where AI generation is genuinely safe and genuinely differentiating.
Not legal advice

This is a risk-reduction framework built on documented enforcement patterns, not a legal opinion. If you scale this into a real business, spend a few hundred dollars on an hour with an IP lawyer in your jurisdiction. Cheapest insurance you'll ever buy.

The seven formats

Format 01

The Countdown Authority

One account whose entire identity is the countdown. Daily post: days remaining plus one genuine piece of news or context. Serialized content builds return viewers, and return viewers are what algorithms reward. Every post has a built-in reason to exist, with honest urgency baked in.

Revenue speed MediumCost Very lowLegal risk Very lowPost-wave value Low
Format 02

The Preparation Channel

Everything a buyer needs to decide and prepare. Which edition. Console comparisons. Storage. Preload timing. Budgeting for an $80 game. What to do if you're on PC and there's no PC version. This is commercial-intent content — someone watching "which edition should I buy" is 30 seconds from a purchase. Highest-value viewer on the internet, best affiliate economics of the seven.

Revenue speed FastCost LowLegal risk Very lowPost-wave value Medium
Format 03

The Guide Machine

Post-launch how-to at volume. Every mechanic, every question, answered in 30–60 seconds. Longest tail of any format — a guide published in November 2026 still gets views in 2028. Converts best to long-form, which is where the real CPMs live.

Revenue speed SlowCost MediumLegal risk LowPost-wave value Very high
Format 04

The Industry Analyst

The business story. Pre-order revenue. Fiscal-year targets. Other studios moving release dates. Retail impact. The economics of an $80 game. The end of physical discs. Older, wealthier, more Western audience = dramatically higher CPMs, and far less competition because it requires you to actually think.

Revenue speed MediumCost MediumLegal risk Very lowPost-wave value Very high
Format 05

The Aesthetic Channel

Pure vibe. AI-generated neon Miami, sunset causeways, 80s Florida. No characters, no logos, no claims. Music-driven, no voiceover, infinitely scalable. Rides the cultural wave, not the IP — nobody owns Miami at night. Monetizes through music, prints, wallpaper packs, and the aesthetic-account economy.

Revenue speed SlowCost Very lowLegal risk ~ZeroPost-wave value Medium
Format 06

The Reaction / Compilation Desk

Aggregating and commenting on the discourse. Best theories, funniest takes, streamer reactions, what people got right. Infinite raw material, fastest possible production. But this carries the highest legal exposure of the seven — you're using other people's footage. Substantial commentary, short excerpts, credited sources. Never a straight repost.

Revenue speed FastCost Very lowLegal risk MediumPost-wave value Low
Format 07

The Money Channel

Content about making money around the launch, aimed at other creators. Best monetization of any format because the audience buys business education. Caution: unsubstantiated earnings claims give you an FTC problem and an ad-account problem. Talk about method, not money.

Revenue speed FastCost LowLegal risk LowPost-wave value High

Section 04

The Stack

Every beginner's instinct is to assemble the perfect toolkit. Wrong. Every hour evaluating tools is an hour not publishing. One tool per job, locked for 116 days.

The seven jobs

01 — Research and script

An AI assistant is your writing partner, not your writer. The workflow that actually produces good scripts: feed it your raw material and your angle; ask for structure not prose; rewrite the hook yourself, always (it's 80% of performance and AI hooks are recognizably generic); read it aloud and rewrite anything you stumble on.

Rule

Never publish a script you haven't touched. Pure AI output underperforms and viewers can feel it.

02 — Voice

Text-to-speech has crossed the quality threshold. Pick one voice and never change it — voice consistency is your brand identity in faceless content. Avoid the default narrator voices everyone uses; audiences are fatigued by them and they signal low effort. Slight imperfection beats perfection: natural pauses, varied pace. The $5–$25/month tier is fine.

03 — Visuals

Three sources, in order of preference: original AI-generated footage (generic scenes — safe and distinctive); licensed stock; short excerpts of official promotional material with commentary. Never AI content designed to look like real gameplay. Generate in batches of 20, keep the best 8. A 45-second video is 6–9 clips.

04 — Assembly

One editor. Learn its shortcuts. Build three templates and reuse them forever: (A) talking-points, (B) list/ranking, (C) countdown/news. Templates are the entire game — a templated video takes 12 minutes, an untemplated one takes 90.

05 — Captions

Auto-captions, always on, burned in. Non-negotiable — most short-form is watched muted. Style once, save the preset, never touch again.

06 — Thumbnails (long-form only)

Three templates. High contrast, one face or object, 3–5 words max. No logos, no official art, no trademarked typography.

07 — Scheduling

A scheduler is the difference between a hobby and a machine. Batch Sunday, schedule the week, spend weekdays on research and comments only.

The 20-minute production loop

Time it. Over 25 minutes means your templates aren't tight enough.

00:00–00:04   Pull one item from your research doc
00:04–00:09   Draft script (AI-assisted), rewrite the hook yourself
00:09–00:12   Generate voiceover, listen once, regenerate if flat
00:12–00:17   Drop into template, swap b-roll, adjust timing
00:17–00:19   Auto-caption, quick styling pass
00:19–00:20   Export, queue with title / description / hashtags

Batch day: Sunday, 4 hours = 12–15 videos = a full week of daily posting across two platforms, with buffer.

The research doc — your unfair advantage

Open one document. Call it INVENTORY. Every news item, stat, good comment-section question, or community argument becomes one line in it. Keep 30+ unused ideas at all times; below 15, you spend a session refilling.

Why this matters more than it sounds

Never sit down to make content and have to think of what to make. That's where the hours die. Separate idea capture from production completely — different parts of your brain, and mixing them is why people burn out in week three.

Section 05

Hooks & Retention

Not likes. Not follows. Watch time as a percentage of length — and specifically retention in the first three seconds.

Every algorithm on every platform optimizes for one thing: keeping the user on the app. Your video is a candidate for that job. Retention is your application.

Target: short-form under 45 seconds should hold 60%+ average view duration. Below 45%, the video is dead — don't try to save it, make a better one.

The six hook patterns

You have three literal seconds before the thumb moves.

1 — Open a loop

State something incomplete that requires the rest to resolve.
"There's one edition almost nobody should buy, and it's not the cheap one."

2 — Contradict an assumption

Take the default belief and reverse it.
"Everyone's telling you to pre-order now. Here's why waiting until November 12 is objectively better."

3 — Deliver a number

Specific numbers are inherently credible and inherently curious.
"Four other studios moved their release dates to get out of the way of one game."

4 — Name the enemy

Give the audience something to be against.
"The reason there's no PC version at launch has nothing to do with development difficulty."

5 — Create time pressure

Real deadlines only.
"You have 116 days, and three things matter in the first week."

6 — Ask the question they're already asking

Mirror the search query directly.
"Is the $99 edition actually worth twenty extra dollars?"

The retention structure

0:00–0:03   HOOK        the six patterns above
0:03–0:08   STAKES      why this matters to them specifically
0:08–0:35   PAYOFF      deliver, but hold one detail back
0:35–0:42   OPEN LOOP   the held-back detail, teased
0:42–0:45   CTA         one action, one only

Three techniques that measurably lift retention

  • Pattern interrupt every 4–6 seconds. A cut, zoom, text pop, sound. Not decoration — a reset of the attention clock.
  • Never front-load context. Start in the middle of the interesting part. Explain after you've earned attention.
  • Never say "in this video I'll…" You've just told them they can leave and come back. They won't come back.

The CTA problem

One CTA. Ever. "Follow for the daily countdown" or "link in bio" or "comment X" — never two.

The highest-converting faceless CTA

The comment-gate: "Comment WINDOW and I'll send you the checklist." It drives comments (algorithmic signal), opens a DM thread (platform-native, no external-link penalty), and a DM is a direct line to an email capture. This one mechanic is how most faceless operators build lists.

The 60/30/10 mix

  • 60% volume content — your reliable templated format. Your floor.
  • 30% swing content — bigger idea, riskier hook. Most flop. One in ten changes your month.
  • 10% conversion content — explicitly sells. Only earned once the other 90% built trust.

Most people do 100% volume and wonder why nothing takes off. A few do 100% swing and burn out. The mix is the strategy.

Section 06

The Machine

Faceless content is a portfolio business. Any individual video is a lottery ticket with unknown odds. The only reliable input is number of tickets.

PhaseShorts/dayLong-form/wkPlatforms
Phase 1 (now–Oct 15)2–312
Phase 2 (Oct 15–Nov 11)4–623
Phase 3 (Nov 12–Dec 31)6–1033+

If those feel impossible, they are — without templates and batching. With them, Phase 1 is about 45 minutes a day plus a 4-hour Sunday.

The weekly rhythm

Sunday — BATCH (4 hrs)

Refill INVENTORY to 30+. Write 12–15 scripts. Generate all voiceovers. Assemble all videos. Schedule the entire week.

Mon–Fri — OPERATE (30–45 min/day)

Check yesterday's metrics. Respond to every comment in the first hour after posting — this materially affects distribution. Capture new INVENTORY items. Handle DMs and list capture.

Saturday — REVIEW (1 hr)

Pull the week's numbers. Identify your top 2 and bottom 2. Ask one question: what did the top two have in common? Feed the answer into next week's batch.

That's the whole operating system. Roughly 10 hours a week.

Multi-account structure

Three accounts minimum, differentiated not identical:

  • A — Primary. Best format, your brand, your monetization. Most effort.
  • B — Variant. Same niche, different format. Tests whether the format or the niche is what's working.
  • C — Experimental. Where you test hooks you're not confident in.

Separate emails, separate devices or clean browser profiles, never cross-post identical files, don't link them publicly until one clearly wins. The point isn't gaming anything — it's redundancy and A/B testing.

Reading your numbers

Weekly, not daily. Daily checking makes you change things too fast and you never learn anything.

MetricHealthyIf below
3-second retention70%+Hook problem. Only fix hooks for two weeks.
Avg view duration60%+Body problem. Cut length by 30%.
Follows / 1k views5+Identity problem. Unclear what your account is.
Comments / 1k views3+No reason to engage. Add questions and gates.
Link CTR1%+CTA problem. One CTA, stated twice, on screen.
The one rule of iteration

Change one variable per week. Change three and you learn nothing.

Burnout is the actual failure mode

Almost nobody fails because the strategy was wrong. They fail because they stopped in week five when they'd made $14.

  1. Decouple effort from results. Your Phase 1 job is publishing, not earning. Grade yourself on posts shipped. Dollars are a Phase 2–3 metric.
  2. The batch is sacred. If Sunday happens, the week happens. Protect that block harder than anything else.
  3. Expect the week-five wall. Around day 30–40 this will feel pointless. That feeling is scheduled and universal, and it arrives right before compounding starts. Know it's coming and you'll walk through it.
Section 07

The Money

Seven rails, ranked by speed to first dollar. Run several at once — that's the whole point of "stack."

Rail 01 · Fastest

Affiliate commissions

Not the game — publisher margins are thin to nonexistent. Everything around it: consoles and bundles, controllers, headsets, capture cards, storage expansion (huge — modern titles are enormous and it's a high-intent purchase), chairs, monitors, TVs, gift cards, subscriptions.

Zero product creation, works day one, and "which edition / what do I need" content is already commercial-intent traffic. One link in bio, a simple page with 8–10 recommendations, content that naturally arrives at a recommendation. Disclose the relationship — legally required and it costs almost nothing in conversion.

Rail 02

Platform creator programs

Enroll in every one you qualify for — but set expectations correctly: supplementary income, not the business. Payouts vary enormously by platform, audience geography, and category, and gaming/youth audiences trend low. The exception is long-form YouTube, where ad revenue is an order of magnitude better per view. That's why shorts are advertising for your long-form.

Rail 03 · Highest margin

Your own digital product

Where the real money is, and the rail almost nobody runs because it feels hard. It isn't. A preparation guide ($9–19). A launch-week strategy guide ($19–29, sold in the 10 days before release). Wallpaper and aesthetic packs ($5–12, near-zero cost with AI generation). A paid notification service — Discord or SMS for news the moment it breaks ($5/mo). Post-launch guide compilations ($19–39).

Margin is ~97%. Ten sales a day of a $19 product is $5,700 a month from an audience of a few thousand. Critical: your product must not use the IP. Same rules as Section 02.

Rail 04 · Highest per unit

Brand deals and UGC

Accessory brands, energy drinks, VPNs, mobile games, hardware makers all spend heavily in this window because they know where the attention is going. You don't need a million followers — brands buy audience quality and relevance, and a 15k account with a defined, high-intent audience in a launch window can command $200–$800 per post.

UGC is the underrated version: you make content for them to post on their channels, and you need no audience at all. $150–$500 per video. Start outreach in early October — budgets get allocated ahead of the window. Appendix E has the exact emails to send.

Rail 05

Traffic redirection

Your short-form audience is a raw resource. Move it somewhere worth more: shorts → long-form (10–20× revenue per view); shorts → email list (sell repeatedly, forever); shorts → Discord (community, recurring revenue); shorts → a niche site or newsletter carrying its own ads.

The email list is the single most valuable asset you'll build in 116 days. Platform reach is rented; the list is owned.

Rail 06 · Largest single payout

Selling the asset

Aged, monetized, topically relevant accounts get bought and sold, typically at 12–30× monthly profit depending on platform, niche, and revenue transferability. A channel doing $2,000/month in verifiable revenue by early 2027 is plausibly a $24,000–$60,000 asset.

To make it saleable: clean books from day one, revenue not dependent on your face or personality (faceless is a feature here), documented processes, and no IP-infringement history in the account's record.

Rail 07

Teaching the method

Once you have a system that works, the system is a product. Legitimate when — and only when — you've actually done the thing.

Hard line

Don't sell "how to make money" before you've made money. Not for the ethics lecture — because it gives you FTC exposure, ad-account bans, chargebacks, and a merchant-account shutdown that kills every other rail simultaneously. Earn the right first.

The calendar

Nov 12 — Preload day

Preload opens. First real surge. Content: "Preload is live — here's exactly what to do." Storage requirements, download size, common errors.

Highest affiliate day of the campaign

People are discovering right now that they don't have enough storage. Have the recommendations ready and pinned.

Nov 13–18 — The approach

Peak anticipation. Review embargo likely lifts. Early impressions land. Daily countdown, review roundups, last-minute prep, launch-night plans. Volume: 6–8 posts/day — the steepest part of the curve. Push your product hard here; buying intent peaks before launch, not after.

Nov 19 — Launch day

The largest single day of consumer attention in gaming history. Go live if you can. Post every 2–3 hours: server status, install issues, first impressions, early tips. Have 10 videos pre-made and scheduled so you can spend the day reacting to what actually happens instead of producing.

Nov 20–26 — The first week

Everyone's playing. Everyone's stuck on something. Pivot hard to guides. Every question in your comments is a video — mine your own comment section, it's the best keyword research tool you'll ever have.

Nov 27–30 — Black Friday

The commercial peak of the year collides with the peak of the wave. Deals content, accessory recommendations, bundle comparisons. Your single highest-revenue affiliate window of the year. Prepare it in early November.

Dec 1–24 — The gift window

Purchase intent stays elevated. Console bundles, gift cards, accessories. Gift guides, "what to get the person who just got the game," continued guides.

Dec 25–31 — The second wave

Millions of new consoles are opened on the 25th. A brand-new cohort of first-time players arrives, all needing beginner content. Most creators are exhausted and coasting by now. Excellent traffic, almost no competition.

Phase 3 discipline

  1. No new formats. You test in Phase 1, you execute in Phase 3.
  2. No new platforms. Adding one in November means starting a cold account during your highest-value window.
  3. No days off. 50 days. Rest in January. This is the whole point of the preceding 80.
Section 09

The Pivot

The mistake almost everyone makes: they build a channel about one event, the event ends, the traffic dies, and they delete it and start over — throwing away the asset in the exact month it became valuable.

By January you'll have something you couldn't buy: an aged, monetized, topically authoritative channel with a real audience and a track record. Aged accounts get better distribution than new ones. That's the asset.

Four viable pivots

Pivot 01 · Easiest

Go deeper

Stay on the same subject. Post-launch has years of runway: the online component, updates, DLC, and the PC port when it arrives — historically 6–18 months later, likely sometime in 2027, and it will be its own launch event with its own wave. Best for Format 03 guide channels.

Pivot 02

Go wider

Broaden from one title to the category. "The launch analysis channel" becomes "the gaming analysis channel." Your audience is already gaming-interested; you're just expanding the subject. Best for Format 04 analyst channels.

Cadence

20 emails in week one of October. One follow-up each after 5–7 days. Expect 2–4 real conversations per 20 sends — that's a normal hit rate, not a failure. Stop following up after one bump; move the energy to the next 20.
